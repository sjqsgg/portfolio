## Confirmed overrides — 17 September 2026

These decisions supersede conflicting exploratory directions later in this document:

- The modeled asset is the workstation itself, not a surrounding room.
- The board direction is felt rather than a perforated pegboard. Only the scratch card is committed content for the first detailed board pass; other board objects are deferred.
- Audio equipment remains part of the workstation, but checkpoint 01 establishes a lighter, less crowded direction with a narrower audio module and smaller speakers.
- The earlier dark-green-heavy palette is rejected. The active palette exploration uses pale yellow-green, warm cream/ash and restrained metal; exact colours remain provisional until material and lighting passes are complete.
- Isabelle is not humanised or resized into adult proportions. She retains her original compact Animal Crossing body and begins low in the chair, potentially with only a small amount visible.
- The target material language is stylised commercial 3D rather than photorealism: simplified large forms, broad controlled bevels, satin surfaces, restrained reflective metal and deliberate studio lighting.
- Workstation development now follows the [round harness](./WORKSTATION-HARNESS.md). [Checkpoint 01](./workstation-lookdev-round-01.md) is the baseline for round 02.

## Design read

Reading this as a dual-practice personal portfolio for technology recruiters, potential collaborators, and photography clients. The site should feel spatial, precise, personal, retro-modern, and materially believable. It should lean toward a designed working environment rather than a conventional portfolio template.

Design mode: redesign overhaul. Preserve the useful content and existing identity, but replace the visual language and homepage structure.

- Design variance: 8/10. Asymmetric, object-led, and compositionally bold.
- Motion intensity: 7/10. Choreographed and tactile, but always purposeful.
- Visual density: 5/10. A lived-in desk with many believable objects, while preserving hierarchy and breathing room.

## Core decision

This will be one website for two equally important identities:

- Software engineer focused on software, AI, automation, problem discovery, and product creation.
- Photographer developing a professional practice in events, portraits, and documentary work.

The homepage is the shared threshold. The two identities branch into separate content paths, but they belong to one person and one visual world.

The homepage concept is: **a real working space waking up**.

## Reference synthesis

### Hiroto Sato

Keep:

- One memorable modeled object system occupying the page.
- Immediate spatial impact.
- A light, quiet environment that gives the model room to feel premium.
- Clear interactive destinations connected to physical objects.
- A small playful interaction layer that rewards curiosity.

Do not copy:

- The utility-pole silhouette.
- Road-sign typography placed directly on the object.
- Sticker interaction as a literal exact replica.

Translation for this site:

- Perspective, diagonal depth, strategic cropping, the lamp, and the pegboard create vertical and spatial impact.
- External HTML labels with leader lines replace words printed on road signs.
- Personal traces can later appear through a guestbook, notes, or limited pegboard interaction.

### Gionatan Nese

Keep:

- Confident art direction.
- Strong hierarchy between the primary experience and supporting information.
- Deliberate page transitions and motion that reveal structure.

### Paul Kalkbrenner

Keep:

- Atmospheric presentation.
- Media treated as part of the environment, not as generic cards.
- A strong relationship between sound, state, and visual mood.

## Homepage composition

### Overall framing

- The 3D workbench is nearly full-screen and borderless.
- Do not put it inside a gray rectangle, browser-like panel, or conventional hero card.
- The scene should feel as if it extends beyond the viewport.
- Use strategic cropping instead of shrinking the full desk to fit.
- The visual direction runs approximately from bottom-left to top-right.
- Perspective and depth should replace the vertical drama that Hiroto gets from a utility pole.
- The name, short role description, and minimal navigation sit around the edges of the scene.
- Text must never compete with the workbench for visual dominance.

### Camera behavior

- No automatic continuous rotation.
- Pointer drag or touch drag rotates the view.
- Horizontal orbit is limited to approximately 25-35 degrees in each direction.
- Vertical orbit is limited to approximately 8-12 degrees.
- The default camera angle must already look composed and complete.
- Rotation reveals useful secondary objects. It should not expose an unfinished back side.
- On small screens, use a shallower orbit and a tighter curated crop.

### Full-screen visual impact

The workbench does not need a naturally tall central object. Its impact comes from:

- The L-shaped desk receding in perspective.
- A rear modular storage system rising behind it.
- A pegboard creating a vertical working layer.
- A tall vintage lamp marking the corner.
- Foreground objects being cropped by the viewport edge.
- A clear near, middle, and far plane.
- Controlled negative space for interface labels.

## Workbench architecture

### Structural reference

Use the later blue-and-white Herman Miller-style workstation as the main structural reference:

- L-shaped desk.
- Thin work surfaces rather than an oversized thick block.
- Chrome or slim metal frame.
- Modular storage behind the work surface.
- Realistic adult furniture proportions.
- The structure can be visually large because useful and personal objects will occupy it.

The final design may borrow the light modularity of the blue-and-white reference without reproducing its color literally.

### Material direction

Acceptable material families:

- Pale, nearly white wood combined with brushed or polished metal.
- Powder-coated color panels combined with chrome tubing.
- Off-white laminate, pale blue, muted green, and a small dark contrast.

Avoid:

- Dark brown wood.
- Chunky toy-like proportions.
- Uniform plastic materials.
- Excessively rounded children's-furniture styling.
- Empty decorative compartments with objects placed at implausible scales.

### Proportion rules

- Every object uses a believable real-world scale.
- The desk is sized for a person, not sized around the objects.
- Shelves and compartments are determined by storage logic.
- Camera equipment should not occupy an artificially oversized plinth.
- The photography cabinet and camera mat must shrink when the number of cameras or lenses is reduced.
- Objects should form natural clusters with usable clear space around them.

## Spatial zoning

The two identities are clearly separated at a large scale, but smaller objects soften the boundary.

### Developer side

Primary cluster:

- One external monitor.
- Keyboard.
- Mouse.
- No MacBook in the hero scene. It is redundant once the monitor, keyboard, and mouse establish the workstation.

Supporting items:

- CV stored in a document folder or represented by a work badge.
- Notebook and pen.
- Project material.
- Headphones on the pegboard.
- Shanxi map project, either on the pegboard or grouped with other projects. Its final placement remains open.

### Photography side

Primary cluster:

- One Sony digital camera rather than two digital bodies.
- One film camera.
- One instant camera or CCD camera, selected later according to silhouette balance.
- A compact row of lenses, reduced by at least one from the earlier concept.
- Optional flash placed with the camera equipment.
- No large softbox in the homepage scene.

Supporting items:

- Existing photography business card.
- Small camera accessories at realistic scale.
- Photography books or a contact sheet.

The photography cabinet should be visibly shorter than in the previous concept. Removing an object must also remove its reserved empty space.

### Shared corner

The corner is the transition zone between both identities:

- Green vintage table lamp with chrome vertical rods and cylindrical shade.
- Pegboard or perforated panel behind the desk.
- Ceramic mug rather than a stainless-steel bottle.
- Notebook or guestbook.
- A few crossing objects can visually bridge the developer and photography zones.

The lamp should sit naturally beside the pegboard and must not obscure its useful area.

### Pegboard

Purpose:

- Adds vertical structure.
- Gives small objects a logical home.
- Provides an interaction layer without enlarging the base cabinet.

Possible contents:

- Work badge or CV badge.
- Map or project print.
- Headphones.
- Small tools, clips, or cable loop.
- A limited number of visitor notes in a later version.

The entire bottom cabinet does not need to become taller. Prefer a dedicated raised rear panel or vertical frame at the corner. The panel should feel attached to the furniture structure rather than placed in front of it.

### Audio zone

- Use recognizable vintage speakers with visible drivers.
- Large speakers may occupy the lower photography-side cabinet area.
- Speaker proportions should be substantial enough to read immediately as speakers.
- A receiver, turntable, or record storage can be added if it helps the composition.
- Audio never starts automatically. Playback requires an explicit click.
- A visible mute or pause control is mandatory.

### Guestbook

- The notebook or message board sits on a stand.
- Move it inward from the outer desk edge.
- The angle should suggest that a visitor could comfortably write in it.
- It may open an HTML guestbook overlay rather than accepting text directly inside WebGL.
- Production implementation needs validation, spam protection, and moderation.

### Secondary lived-in objects

Candidates include:

- Travel maps.
- Project maps.
- A vase.
- A small plant.
- A ceramic mug or water reminder.
- An umbrella beside the storage frame.
- Records.
- A few picture books or photography books.
- Pens and a notebook.

These should make the desk believable, not become a checklist. Each object needs a spatial reason and a hierarchy level.

## Interaction hierarchy

### Primary entrances

Only two objects are primary navigation targets:

1. Monitor: Developer.
2. Camera: Photography.

Both must be discoverable within the first few seconds without requiring random exploration.

### Primary labels

The objects themselves should not carry oversized section text. Use crisp HTML labels tied to each object with a fine leader line.

Developer label:

```text
BUILD THINGS
Developer projects
Software, AI & automation
```

Photography label:

```text
FRAME MOMENTS
Photography
Events, portraits & documentary
```

The precise copy can be refined, but the functional hierarchy should remain:

- Short active phrase.
- Clear destination name.
- One concise scope line.

### Entry choreography

Suggested timing:

1. 0-1.0 seconds: the camera and workbench settle into position; name appears.
2. 1.0-2.0 seconds: monitor wakes with a restrained screen change.
3. 2.0-2.7 seconds: camera status light activates and the lens catches one controlled glint.
4. 2.7-3.5 seconds: leader lines and the two labels appear.
5. After the first interaction: verbose help fades, leaving only the functional labels.
6. After approximately 8 seconds of inactivity: show a one-time drag or select hint.

Suggested one-time hint:

```text
Drag to look around. Select an object.
```

The sequence communicates hierarchy and affordance. It is not decorative animation.

### Hover and focus behavior

For the monitor and camera:

- Show `OPEN` or `VIEW` close to the relevant label.
- Allow approximately 1-2 degrees of physical response.
- Darken or increase the contrast of the associated label.
- Use a slight local light or material response.
- Do not use a permanent neon glow.
- Cursor, keyboard focus, and touch states must lead to the same destination.

### Click transitions

- Clicking the monitor opens the Developer index.
- Clicking the camera opens the Photography index.
- Use a short camera move or object-focused transition before the route changes.
- Keep the transition under roughly 800 milliseconds unless loading requires longer.
- Reduced-motion mode uses an immediate fade or direct navigation.

### Secondary interactions

Secondary objects reveal themselves only on hover, focus, or after slight scene rotation:

- Lamp: Day/Night.
- Badge or folder: CV.
- Business card: contact details or photography contact.
- Map: project or travel story.
- Notebook: guestbook.
- Speaker, receiver, or record player: audio.

Secondary labels are quieter and must never compete with the two primary destinations.

### Optional playful interaction

A later iteration can allow visitors to leave a small visual trace:

- Place a limited note on the pegboard.
- Add a restrained stamp to the guestbook.
- Reveal previously approved visitor notes.

Do not duplicate Hiroto's sticker mechanic exactly. The interaction should make sense in a working studio and should not clutter the first visit.

## Day and night system

### Lamp as physical control

- Clicking the lamp switches the entire site between Day and Night modes.
- The lamp is not merely switching the 3D scene.
- Its physical light, background, UI colors, and supporting pages change together.
- The selected mode persists between visits.
- Initial mode respects `prefers-color-scheme` unless a saved choice exists.

### Accessible parallel control

- Provide a conventional HTML theme toggle in the interface.
- Both controls reflect the same state.
- The lamp has an accessible name such as `Switch to night mode`.
- Theme switching must not depend on animation or color alone.

### Light mode

- Hiroto-like pale spatial background.
- Pale furniture surfaces.
- Soft chrome reflections.
- Near-black text rather than pure black.
- Green lamp and selected green details can connect the site to the photography business card.

### Night mode

- Preserve the same material identity and hierarchy.
- Allow the lamp, monitor, and small equipment lights to become more visible.
- Avoid generic blue-purple cyberpunk lighting.
- Do not turn every object into an emissive element.

## Color direction

The palette is not fully locked. Current strongest direction:

- Pale neutral background.
- Off-white or very pale wood.
- Chrome or cool silver frame.
- Muted green as the primary accent, tied to the business card and lamp.
- Pale blue may appear as a supporting storage-panel color.
- Dark charcoal for monitor, cameras, lenses, and typography.

Alternative still worth testing:

- Blue-and-white modular workstation with green used only for the lamp and small brand markers.

Do not use green and blue at equal intensity. One is the accent and the other is structural support.

## Typography and interface layer

- English is the primary site language.
- Use a confident modern sans-serif with a restrained mono companion for metadata.
- Interface labels remain HTML, not baked into textures.
- Navigation stays on one line on desktop.
- Labels should be short and functional rather than poetic or cryptic.
- Do not use large floating headings that obscure the 3D model.
- Do not use decorative section numbers, fake build metadata, or unnecessary scroll instructions.

## Information architecture

Current page set:

- Home.
- Developer index.
- Developer project detail.
- Photography index.
- Photography series detail.
- About and Contact.

Homepage object mapping:

- Monitor to Developer index.
- Camera to Photography index.
- Badge or folder to CV.
- Business card to Contact or photography contact.
- Map to the Shanxi map project or a project story.
- Notebook to Guestbook.
- Lamp to theme.
- Audio equipment to optional music playback.

## Motion and implementation rules

- Animate transforms, opacity, material values, and lights. Avoid layout-driven animation.
- Honor `prefers-reduced-motion`.
- Never update React state on every pointer or scroll frame.
- Three.js or React Three Fiber owns the scene motion.
- GSAP may coordinate the entry timeline and route transitions if it remains isolated from the render loop.
- HTML labels follow projected 3D anchor points and remain legible at every supported viewport.
- Audio is user initiated.
- The main scene should lazy-load noncritical high-resolution textures and secondary meshes.
- The first meaningful view must remain useful while the full model loads.

## Performance targets

- LCP under 2.5 seconds on a representative mobile connection.
- INP under 200 milliseconds.
- CLS under 0.1.
- Reserve the scene's dimensions before WebGL loads.
- Use compressed GLB, KTX2 or WebP/AVIF textures where appropriate, and Draco or Meshopt after verifying visual quality.
- Keep separate level-of-detail strategies for desktop and mobile.
- Provide a static fallback image when WebGL is unavailable or reduced-data mode is active.

## Blender and GLB production path

1. Keep the current gray model as the spatial and proportion test.
2. Lock the final camera angle and orbit limits before detailed modeling.
3. Correct desk thickness, cabinet length, and object scale.
4. Build one representative look-development zone first.
5. Test pale wood or laminate, chrome, green enamel, dark camera materials, and speaker wood.
6. Establish one light-mode rig and one night-mode rig.
7. Separate interactive objects into named meshes with stable origins.
8. Create simple collision or interaction proxies where needed.
9. Optimize topology and texture resolution according to screen importance.
10. Export a web-ready GLB and test it in the actual homepage viewport before finishing secondary details.

Suggested interactive mesh names:

```text
INT_monitor
INT_camera_primary
INT_lamp
INT_badge_cv
INT_business_card
INT_map_shanxi
INT_guestbook
INT_audio
```

## Figma prototype requirements

The Figma homepage should communicate:

- Nearly full-screen, borderless scene.
- Diagonal composition and controlled cropping.
- Approximate locations of monitor, camera, lamp, pegboard, audio area, and guestbook.
- Two dominant leader-line labels.
- Lamp theme affordance.
- Entry timeline annotation.
- Default, hover/focus, Day, and Night states.
- Desktop and mobile crops.

It does not need to reproduce detailed 3D materials. Its job is to validate hierarchy, labels, responsive framing, and interaction sequence before further Blender work.

## Validation checklist

- Can a first-time visitor identify Developer and Photography within five seconds?
- Do the monitor and camera look clickable without permanent glow?
- Does the scene look like a real adult workspace rather than a toy set?
- Are furniture and object proportions plausible?
- Does the photography cabinet shrink with its reduced equipment list?
- Is the lamp visible without blocking the pegboard?
- Is the guestbook placed where a person could actually use it?
- Are speakers immediately recognizable?
- Does the desk retain thin, elegant surfaces?
- Does the default view feel composed before the visitor drags?
- Are primary and secondary interactions visually distinct?
- Does Day/Night affect the entire site consistently?
- Does the page remain understandable with reduced motion and without WebGL?

## Open decisions

These should be tested rather than assumed:

- Whether pale blue or pale wood is the dominant structural surface.
- Whether the third photography body is an instant camera or a CCD camera.
- Whether the Shanxi map is displayed on the pegboard or introduced inside the Developer project collection.
- Whether the audio system includes only speakers and a receiver or also a record player.
- Whether visitor notes become a public pegboard feature after launch.
- Exact typefaces and final English copy.

## Explicitly rejected directions

- Separate websites for the two identities at this stage.
- A sparse generic portfolio hero.
- A thick oversized desk made large only to fit all objects.
- Dark wood as the dominant material.
- A model that looks cheap, childish, or excessively rounded.
- Multiple objects placed at arbitrary scale inside unrelated compartments.
- MacBook plus external monitor, keyboard, and mouse in the same hero setup.
- Large softbox in the homepage composition.
- Continuous auto-rotation.
- Permanent glowing hotspots.
- Primary navigation hidden behind unguided object hunting.
- Audio autoplay.
